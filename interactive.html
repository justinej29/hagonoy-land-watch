The basics

Land subsidence is the gradual sinking of the ground surface, or its sudden collapse, caused by the movement of earth materials below it (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-c). It can happen slowly over years or, in some cases, abruptly, and the rate depends on the cause (Galloway et al., 1999).

In Hagonoy, the main cause is the pumping of too much groundwater from underground aquifers. When water is removed from the spaces between soil particles, those particles compact and the ground above sinks (Galloway & Burbey, 2011).

No. An earthquake is the shaking of the ground caused by a sudden release of energy, most often from movement along faults in the Earth's crust (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-d). Land subsidence is a different process: the ground surface sinks because of changes in the materials below it, and it is often gradual enough that it is not felt (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-c). The damage from subsidence builds up over years or decades.

Subsidence is the ground itself getting lower. Flooding is water covering land that is normally dry. They are different problems, but they make each other worse.

When land sinks, the same high tide or heavy rain reaches farther, floods deeper and takes longer to drain. In low-lying coastal and delta areas, sinking land raises flood exposure even when sea level itself has not changed (Syvitski et al., 2009), and coastal cities that are sinking face growing flood risk (Erkens et al., 2015).

Not completely. Much of the compaction caused by groundwater pumping is permanent, so the land rarely returns to its original level. However, the rate of sinking can be slowed or stopped by reducing groundwater extraction and allowing water levels in the aquifer to recover (Galloway & Burbey, 2011).

In cities where groundwater pumping was restricted, such as Tokyo, Japan, subsidence slowed markedly after water levels recovered (Galloway & Burbey, 2011). Prevention is always better than cure.

Hagonoy and your home

Hagonoy sits on soft, low-lying land in the delta of the Pampanga River. Several factors combine to make it especially vulnerable:

  • Soft alluvial soil (the land is made of loose river sediment that compacts easily)
  • High groundwater use (farming, fishponds, and households all depend on wells)
  • Low elevation (parts of Hagonoy are already near or below sea level)
  • Rising sea levels (climate change is raising nearby coastal water, worsening the risk of flooding)
  • Heavy seasonal rains (flood events are more damaging when land has sunk)

A 2026 study of Hagonoy and Calumpit measured ground movement with satellite radar (Sentinel-1 InSAR) from 2015 to early 2021. It found sinking of up to 124.51 mm per year (about 12.5 cm a year) near areas with heavy groundwater pumping.

Ground within 500 meters of pumping stations sank faster on average (about 70.22 mm per year) than ground farther away (about 65.31 mm per year), and the difference was statistically significant. The researchers also estimate that Hagonoy's groundwater extraction of about 9 million cubic meters a year may account for around 81 mm per year of sinking.

The study calls for local monitoring and for including subsidence risk in land-use planning. This website does not measure sinking itself, and these figures are not ratings for individual barangays. Source: Maclang et al. (2026).

On the map above, the barangays rated High for flood exposure are Mercado, Pugad, Sagrada Familia, San Miguel, San Nicolas, San Sebastian, Santa Elena, Santa Monica, Santo Niño, Santo Rosario, Tampok and Tibaguin.

Please remember that these ratings come from news reports, and that floods have recently reached every barangay in Hagonoy. A lower rating does not mean an area is safe.

Warning signs that your home or area may be experiencing land subsidence include:

  • Cracks appearing in walls, floors, or ceilings that were not there before
  • Doors and windows that used to close properly now stick or leave gaps
  • Floors that feel uneven or tilted
  • Water pooling in areas that did not flood before
  • Roads or pavements cracking or dipping unevenly

If you notice these signs, document them with photos and report them to your barangay captain. Use the Report form in Section 4 below.

Many families in Hagonoy depend on fishponds and rice farming for their income. Land subsidence affects these livelihoods in several ways:

  • Sinking land makes fishpond dikes harder to maintain (saltwater and floodwater breach them more easily)
  • Rice paddies that sink below drainage level become permanently waterlogged and unproductive
  • Saltwater intrusion into freshwater sources reduces water quality for aquaculture
  • Irrigation infrastructure (canals and gates) becomes misaligned as land sinks unevenly

These losses force families to seek other income, which often increases migration away from the area.

Taking action

Every household action adds up. Start with these steps:

  • Fix leaking pipes (wasted water means more pumping needed)
  • Collect rainwater for non-drinking uses like gardening and washing
  • Reduce paving around your home so rainwater can soak into the ground
  • Plant trees and shrubs whose roots help water infiltrate the soil
  • Report signs of subsidence to barangay officials

See the full checklist on the Solutions page.

If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or contact your local disaster risk reduction and management office (DRRMO).

  • Evacuate to higher ground if flooding is rising quickly
  • Stay away from cracked or sinking structures (they may be unstable)
  • Do not use flooded roads if you cannot see the bottom
  • Contact your barangay emergency team

The Report form below is for non-emergency documentation only, not for emergencies.

Several government agencies share this responsibility:

  • Mines and Geosciences Bureau (MGB) (conducts geohazard assessments and surveys)
  • PHIVOLCS (monitors geological and volcanic hazards across the country)
  • NAMRIA (produces official maps and topographic data)
  • PAGASA (tracks climate and sea level changes that affect subsidence risk)
  • NWRB (regulates groundwater extraction through well permits)
