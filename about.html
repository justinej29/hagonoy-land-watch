Understanding the basics

About Land Subsidence

Land subsidence, known locally as paglubog ng lupa, is the gradual sinking of the ground surface. In Hagonoy, Bulacan, it is one of the most pressing environmental challenges facing the community today.

What it is

Defining Land Subsidence

Land subsidence is the downward movement of the ground surface relative to a fixed reference point, such as sea level. It can be slow and imperceptible, usually only a few millimeters per year, or rapid enough to cause visible structural damage over months.

In low-lying coastal and deltaic areas like Hagonoy, even a few centimeters of subsidence can dramatically increase flood risk, cause saltwater to intrude into freshwater sources, and undermine the foundations of homes and public infrastructure.

Unlike earthquakes or landslides, subsidence is largely invisible until significant damage has already occurred, making monitoring and early awareness critically important.

Key Point: Land subsidence in the Philippines is not a rare event. A study of major Philippine metropolitan areas found its highest sinking rate, up to 109 mm per year, in Bulacan Province (Sulapas et al., 2024). In Hagonoy and Calumpit, subsidence rates between 2015 and the first quarter of 2021 reached 124.51 mm per year near high-extraction zones (Maclang et al., 2026).

The process

How Does It Happen?

1

Groundwater Is Extracted

When water is pumped from underground aquifers faster than it can be replenished through rainfall and natural recharge, the water pressure inside the soil layers decreases significantly. Prolonged groundwater withdrawal reduces the support provided by underground water, increasing the risk of land subsidence (Barlow & Leake, 2012).

2

Soil Layers Compact

Without sufficient water pressure supporting the soil structure, fine-grained sediments such as clay, silt, and peat compress under the weight of the overlying materials. As water is squeezed out of their pore spaces, these sediments become denser, and the compaction may be permanent (Poland & Davis, 1969).

3

Ground Surface Sinks

As the underlying sediment layers compact, the ground surface gradually sinks. In delta regions built on soft sediments, this process is intensified by the natural settling and compression of unconsolidated materials deposited by rivers over thousands of years. Excessive groundwater extraction can further accelerate this process (Galloway et al., 1999).

Local context

Why Is Hagonoy Affected?

Several geographic and socioeconomic factors make Hagonoy, Bulacan particularly vulnerable to land subsidence.

Source: North Manila Bay FPS

Delta Location

Hagonoy sits on the Pampanga River delta, an area built of soft, compressible sediments deposited over centuries. These sediments are naturally prone to settling and compaction.

Source: facebook.com

Heavy Groundwater Use

Many households, fish ponds, and small industries in the area rely on deep wells for their water supply, depleting aquifers and accelerating soil compaction below.

Source: facebook.com

Low Elevation

Much of Hagonoy lies at or near sea level. Even minor subsidence brings areas below the tidal line, making them susceptible to permanent or seasonal flooding.

Source: Rappler.com

Rising Sea Levels

Global sea-level rise compounds subsidence. When the ground sinks and the sea rises simultaneously, the relative difference can be twice as severe as either factor alone.

Recognizing the signs

Warning Signs of Subsidence

Residents and community members can watch for the following indicators that ground subsidence may be occurring in their area.

  • Cracks in walls or floors

    Diagonal or stair-step cracks in masonry that widen over time without an associated earthquake.

  • Doors and windows that no longer close properly

    Frames may shift as the building foundation settles unevenly.

  • Floors that are no longer level

    Noticeable tilting of house floors or outdoor surfaces such as driveways and pathways.

  • Increased flooding frequency

    Areas that did not flood before are now inundated during moderate rain or high tides.

  • Saltwater in wells or fishponds

    Saltwater intrusion into freshwater wells or the brackish takeover of previously freshwater fish ponds.

  • Roads cracking or buckling

    Pavement developing sinkholes, depressions, or waves without apparent cause from heavy traffic alone.

  • Lowered well water levels

    Groundwater becoming harder to reach, requiring deeper wells or pump upgrades to extract.

  • Separation of structures from the ground

    Gaps appearing between building foundations and the surrounding soil, or steps pulling away from thresholds.

Key terminology

Glossary

Understanding these terms will help you follow discussions about land subsidence in scientific reports, news articles, and government documents.

Land Subsidence
The downward movement of the ground surface relative to a fixed datum such as mean sea level. It can be a gradual settling or a sudden sinking, and it can be caused by natural processes or by human activities such as groundwater withdrawal (Galloway et al., 1999).
Aquifer
An underground layer of permeable rock, sediment, or soil that holds groundwater and yields it to wells and springs (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-a). Heavy withdrawal from aquifers is a leading cause of land subsidence (Galloway et al., 1999).
Groundwater
Water held underground in the saturated zone beneath the water table, stored in aquifers (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-a). It is a vital resource for drinking and agriculture, but excessive pumping can cause compaction of the layers above (Poland & Davis, 1969).
Compaction
The process by which soil particles are pressed more closely together, reducing the volume of pore spaces. When groundwater is removed, fine-grained sediments such as clay and silt can compact, and this compaction is often permanent (Poland & Davis, 1969).
Saltwater Intrusion
The movement of saltwater into underground sources of freshwater. Groundwater pumping can reduce the freshwater flow toward the coast and draw saltwater into the freshwater zones of an aquifer (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-b).
Geohazard
A geological condition or process that poses a danger to people, property, or infrastructure. In the Philippines, DENR-MGB assesses and maps geohazards such as rain-induced landslides, floods, and subsidence (Mines and Geosciences Bureau, n.d.).
Delta
A landform created by the deposition of sediment where a river enters a slower or standing body of water such as a bay or sea (National Park Service, n.d.). Deltas are built from soft sediments that can compact over time (Soria et al., 2005).
PHIVOLCS
The Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology, a service institute of the Department of Science and Technology (DOST). It is mandated to mitigate disasters that may arise from volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, tsunamis, and related geotectonic phenomena (Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology, n.d.).
DENR-MGB
The Mines and Geosciences Bureau, an agency under the Department of Environment and Natural Resources. It implements the National Geohazard Assessment and Mapping Program, which produces hazard maps for rain-induced landslides, floods, coastal hazards, and karst subsidence (Mines and Geosciences Bureau, n.d.).
